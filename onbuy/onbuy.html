Marketplace Checkout

OnBuy

Role
UX/UI Designer
Year
2022
Team
Sole designer on the flow, with a product owner, engineers and QA
Tags
Ecommerce, Checkout, Mobile-first, User research

Context

As OnBuy's catalogue grew, the path from finding a product to paying for it started to leak. Search returned overwhelming result sets, product pages buried the buyer-protection signals that earn trust on a marketplace, and a multi-step checkout lost buyers on mobile, where most of the traffic was. The brief said checkout was too long.

Approach

I ran a Userbrain study, recording real people finding a product and trying to buy it, alongside a review of the analytics funnel. The finding reframed the brief: checkout was not too long, it was not trusted. Buyers did not see the cues that told them this was safe until they were already committed.

Over 18 sprints I fixed trust first and friction second, and designed separate paths for buyers who search and buyers who browse.

Rebuilt concept, not the shipped interface.

Key decisions

Trust shows up at the moment of doubt, with buyer protection, delivery and returns beside the price and the pay button. There are two ways in, so search-led and browse-led buyers each get their own layout above the fold. Checkout is one progressive screen with a running summary, so cost and delivery show before the commitment. One thing we cut was information the old flow asked for twice, including address and contact details.

Outcome

A shorter checkout, with cost, delivery and buyer protection shown up front. Mobile checkout completion improved on the platform's highest-traffic flow. The trust-led framework became the team's shared language for later commerce decisions.

The design followed what testers said made them leave, not the brief's first assumption.

Full process

Problem

A marketplace lives on trust and momentum, and both were leaking between basket and confirmation.

Where it hurt

Checkout asked too much, too early, and showed delivery and total cost too late. On mobile, every extra step was another chance to leave.

Who felt it

Buyers comparing listings across the marketplace, and the sellers who lost a sale every time a full basket was abandoned at the last step.

The end goal

Show the reasons to trust before asking for the commitment: cost, delivery and buyer protection up front, with fewer steps.

Research

The product team had flagged buyers dropping out of checkout. An analytics and heuristic review located the drop, then a study on Userbrain recorded real users finding a product and trying to buy it, each leaving a rating and a comment.

Search or browse

What users said, sorted

“I'll add it to the basket, but if I can't see what delivery costs and whether I can send it back, I'll just go and check Amazon instead.”

Usability participant

Discovery

Mapping where buyers leave

The easy read was too many steps, so cut steps. The sessions said something sharper: buyers weren't getting the cues they needed to believe the site was safe, like Trustpilot badges, a secure payment gateway, the company logo and a consistent look all the way to payment. So trust led and friction came second. A three-step checkout still fails if step two feels fraudulent.

A menu built the way shoppers group things

Most products had an obvious home, so a flatter, shopper-led top level was possible. The items that kept jumping groups are listed in both sensible places, with filters doing the rest.
